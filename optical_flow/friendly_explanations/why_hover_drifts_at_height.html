ARK Flow · PAW3902 optical flow

Why hover drifts at height

Position hold on optical flow is tight at 2 m and loose at 20 m, sometimes with a slow rolling oscillation. This note explains the mechanism end to end, puts numbers on each part, and lists what changes it.

In short
  • The flow sensor measures how fast the ground appears to turn under it, in angle. The flight controller multiplies that by height above ground to get speed. Every error in the angle measurement is multiplied by height too.
  • One sensor count is 0.122° of apparent turn: 4 mm of ground at 2 m, 4 cm at 20 m. A hover drift of 0.3 m/s produces a count every other frame at 2 m and one count per 18 frames at 20 m.
  • The flight controller reads each frame as its own measurement and, with today's settings, assumes each one is uncertain by about 6.5 m/s at 20 m. It leans on the accelerometers instead, drifts, then jumps when a count arrives. That is the rolling.
  • Fixes, in order of payoff: average over a longer window when high, make the sensor's quality score mean the same thing in every camera mode, let the flight controller use the range sensor to its full 30 m, and measure the report delay over CAN.

How the sensor measures motion

The PAW3902 on ARK Flow is a motion-tracking chip from the same family as the sensor in an optical mouse, with a lens that focuses on the ground. It takes 126 pictures a second (50 in its lowest-light mode), compares each picture with the previous one, and reports how far the picture slid, as whole counts in x and y. It does not report position or distance. It reports change.

One count is a fixed angle. The datasheet's resolution curve, 11.914 counts per inch per meter of height, works out to 0.122° (2.13 milliradians) per count. The field of view is 42°, so a full sweep across the view is about 344 counts. The chip's resolution register is already at its maximum; there is no setting that makes a count smaller.

Alongside the counts, each report carries a quality score (the number of ground features the chip could track, divided by 4, on a scale of 0–255), the shutter time, and the average brightness. The driver uses shutter and brightness to pick one of three camera modes: Bright, Low Light, or Super Low Light. Switching modes resets the chip and costs about 50 ms of data.

From counts to speed

The flight controller's estimator (EKF2) turns each report into a speed in four steps. The first three are in angle units. Only the last one produces meters.

The subtraction step matters more than it looks. When the drone rolls 1° per second, the picture slides across the sensor exactly as if the ground had moved 1° per second. The gyro on the ARK Flow board records how much the board rotated in the same window, and the flight controller subtracts it. What is left is ground motion. Any mismatch between the two windows, in length or in timing, leaves a residual that the flight controller reads as real motion.

Height multiplies everything before it

A count is an angle, so the ground distance it covers grows with height. So does the speed it represents, and so does every error made in angle units.

At 20 m a slow hover drift is a count every 18 frames. Seventeen frames in a row say "no motion," then one says "one count." Over any window longer than a few tenths of a second the average is exactly right. Frame by frame it is a coin flip.

Three things compound it

1. The reports are quantized, but the noise model is a constant

The estimator does not know that a report is a whole number of counts. It assigns each one a fixed uncertainty in angle units, blended between two settings by the quality score: EKF2_OF_N_MIN (0.15 rad/s, "best quality") and EKF2_OF_N_MAX (0.5 rad/s, "worst"). With the quality scores the sensor actually produces, the blend lands around 0.32 rad/s, or 18° per second. Multiply by height and that is the uncertainty the estimator attaches to every report: 0.65 m/s at 2 m, 6.5 m/s at 20 m.

The real per-frame quantization error is a quarter of that. And it shrinks in proportion to the window: average 50 ms instead of 8 and it drops six-fold. The assumed noise does not shrink at all, because nothing tells the estimator the window changed.

Told that each report is worth ±6.5 m/s, the estimator barely moves on any one of them and carries its velocity on the accelerometers, which drift on their own. Position hold then acts on a drifting velocity. When a count does land, the correction is a lump rather than a nudge. Slow slide, then a lurch.

2. The quality score means different things in different modes

The quality score is a raw feature count. The chip's own datasheet gives a different "unusable below this" floor for each camera mode: 25 in Bright, 70 in Low Light, 85 in Super Low Light. A score of 85 is a decent frame in Bright mode and a barely usable one in Super Low Light. The driver passes the raw number through, so the estimator cannot tell those apart.

Separately, the estimator's trust ramp runs from EKF2_OF_QMIN (default 1) to 255. In the one log we have, Bright-mode scores sit between 90 and 130, which the ramp reads as 35–51% trust. A frame with 500 tracked features is treated as mediocre, and the assumed noise lands near the "worst" end of the blend. That is where the 0.32 rad/s above comes from.

3. Rotation subtraction, timing, and the loop that turns drift into rolling

The subtraction of the drone's own rotation has to be precise in a way it does not at low height. A residual of 0.5°/s (a small gyro bias, or the flow window and gyro window offset by 10 ms during an attitude correction) is 0.02 m/s at 2 m and 0.17 m/s at 20 m. That residual sits inside the position control loop.

Height itself has to be right too. The range sensor on ARK Flow reads to 30 m, but the flight controller only uses it as far as its settings allow (UAVCAN_RNG_MAX, and SENS_FLOW_MAXHGT for flow). Above whatever that cutoff is, the flight controller estimates height above ground from air pressure and from the flow sensor itself. A 2 m error at 20 m is a 10% error in every speed it computes, and letting flow estimate its own scale makes the loop above worse.

What we can do

In order of expected payoff. The first two are the ones that change the picture; the rest close gaps.

  • Average over a longer window when the drone is highflight controller software

    Today the flight controller re-packages flow reports at a fixed rate (SENS_FLOW_RATE). Make the window scale with height instead: about 8 ms at 2 m, about 80 ms at 20 m, targeting a constant amount of ground per report. The run-of-zeros-then-a-spike pattern disappears, and quantization error per report drops in proportion.

    Effect at 20 m: per-report jitter from 1.6 m/s to about 0.16 m/s, at 12 reports per second. The estimator already timestamps each report at the middle of its window, so latency stays honest. A second step is to tell the estimator that noise shrinks with the window, so it stops assuming a constant.

  • Make the quality score mean the same thing in every modeARK Flow firmware + settings

    In the driver, map the raw score onto one scale: 0 at the datasheet floor for the current mode, 255 at a per-mode ceiling measured from flights. Then raise EKF2_OF_QMIN from 1 to a real floor (around 40 on the new scale) so the trust ramp spans what the sensor actually produces.

    Effect: a 500-feature frame is trusted like the good frame it is, and a featureless frame over uniform grass at 20 m is dropped instead of fused at low weight. Needs quality histograms from Low Light and Super Low Light flights; we only have Bright-mode data so far.

  • Let the flight controller use the range sensor to 30 msettings

    Set UAVCAN_RNG_MAX to 30 and SENS_FLOW_MAXHGT at or above the highest altitude you fly on flow. Then check a log from a 20 m hover: the estimator should show range-based terrain active the whole time, and the range sensor's signal quality should hold.

    Effect: speed scale stays measured rather than estimated, and the flow sensor is never asked to work out its own height.

  • Measure the report delay over CAN and set itsettings

    The flight controller stamps each flow report when it arrives, not when the sensor took it, and one setting (EKF2_OF_DELAY) accounts for the difference. The default was chosen for a sensor wired directly to the flight controller. Cross-correlating the ARK Flow's gyro integral against the flight controller's own gyro in a log gives the true delay.

    Effect: a 10 ms timing error is worth 0.17 m/s at 20 m during an attitude correction; removing it weakens the loop in figure 6.

  • Confirm the chip remembers sub-count motionbench test

    Everything above assumes the chip carries motion smaller than one count from frame to frame and reports it when it adds up to a whole count. If it discards it, slow drift at height is invisible and only a narrower lens would help. Rotate the sensor at about 0.6°/s for a minute and check that the total is close to 280 counts.

What will not help

  • Raising the sensor's resolution setting. It is already at maximum.
  • Reporting faster. More reports of the same 0-or-1 count add noise, not information.
  • The illumination LED. It reaches a few meters; at 20 m the sensor runs on ambient light.
  • Tuning the noise settings alone. Without the quality fix and the longer window, lower noise numbers make the estimator believe the spikes.

What to expect

Position hold at 20 m will not match 2 m. The sensor has a tenth of the ground resolution there and that is optics. The goal is a hover that drifts slowly and evenly, without lurches, with an estimator that knows how much each report is worth.
